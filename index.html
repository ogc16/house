Design intent

Why This
Works

The Pavilion is organized around a single spatial idea: the hard separation of social life from private life, joined at a hinge. This is not a stylistic choice — it is a structural one, and everything else follows from it.

In Kampala's climate, cross-ventilation and thermal zoning matter enormously. The bedroom wing faces north, reducing direct sun gain during the day. The living wing faces south and east, capturing morning light and evening breezes. The two-wing structure creates a courtyard effect between them, accelerating air movement through the building naturally.

The open-plan living zone is a deliberate choice for a culture of hospitality — one where guests move between kitchen, dining, and sitting without ceremony, and where a household of many can occupy the same space without crowding any part of it.

01
Separation of zones
Social and sleeping activities are separated by a structural wall and a transitional hallway. Once in the bedroom wing, the house is quiet regardless of activity in the living zone.
02
The office as threshold
Positioning the home office at the hinge means that "going to work" involves a physical transition — not just sitting at a different screen — while still keeping it within the house.
03
Passive climate strategy
The north-facing bedroom wing and south-east-facing living wing work with the equatorial sun rather than against it. No bedroom faces west — avoiding afternoon heat gain in sleeping spaces.
04
Room for growth
The utility strip and office are both over-sized relative to their immediate function. Both can evolve: the office becomes a fourth bedroom, the utility becomes a self-contained studio unit.
